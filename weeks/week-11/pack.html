Shoot like a studio — the frame sells before you speak

Professional-looking content stops the scroll by itself. This week: the shots, angles, and light that get you there on the phone you already own, with about $10 of gear.

The idea

Before you say a word, the frame has already spoken — viewers read "this looks professional" in milliseconds. In a 9:16 vertical frame that comes down to just three decisions: the shot, the angle, the light.

Shot distance = importance. How much of you fills the frame tells the viewer what matters in this moment. And your background is your introduction: if the set says who you are — the gaming rig, the gym, the kitchen — you never waste your opening line on "Hi, I'm…". The frame handles the intro so your first words can hook.

The seven shots

ShotFrameUse it for
Extreme close-upOne detail fills the frameIntimacy, teased reveals, first-frame curiosity
Close-upFace fills the frameEmotion — reads perfectly on small screens
Medium close-upCuts mid-chestTalking, tutorials, story time — face plus hands
Waist-upCuts at the waistBody language, outfits, environment starts to matter
CowboyCuts mid-thighFull outfit with a readable face; movement without feet
Full bodyHead to toeOutfit of the day, choreography, silhouette
WideYou small in the settingWhen the environment is the point

Camera angle = status. Slightly below, the audience looks up at you — elevated, aspirational, in charge. From above — softer, more vulnerable, more intimate. Eye level — a peer talking to a friend. None of the three is wrong; the mistake is letting the tripod decide instead of you.

This week's play No frame is an accident: before every record press, call three things out loud — the shot, the angle, and where the light is coming from (and whether it looks like it comes from there).

Light fills the outline

If a shot feels flat or harsh in a way you can't name, it's almost always the light. Two rules govern everything: expose for the face — everything else can be brighter or darker on purpose — and motivate the light: it should appear to come from a source the viewer can believe (the window side, the lamp side). Then climb the ladder:

SetupHow
One lightKey at 45° to one side, slightly above eye level, angled down — how daylight falls. Kill competing overheads; a ~$10 white board opposite bounces fill back. (A window at 45° works free.)
+ Hair lightSmall light behind and above, at hair and shoulders — a rim that lifts you off the background. The biggest upgrade per dollar.
Full 3-pointAdd fill opposite the key at 50–70% of its intensity — shadows soften, never vanish.
"Cinema-quality images on social media are, in and of themselves, a pattern interrupt."

When we shoot together: a directed shoot day runs this exact standard — a shot list from your content bank, a 30-second frame check before every setup, and direction that says what to change and why it performs, never "do it again." Self-shot weeks, same checklist.

Do / don't

Do

  • Lock exposure manually (Moment app, ~$10) — auto drifts mid-take.
  • Shutter = 1/(2× framerate) — set it, then leave it alone.
  • Match color temps — warm reads intimate, cool reads clinical.

Don't

  • Key dead-center behind the camera — flat passport-photo light.
  • Fill as bright as the key — if both cheeks match, turn it down.
  • Blow out the face — or the white wall behind you.

This week's homework

  1. Film the same clip in three shot types and at least two angles — watch them back to back.
  2. Build the one-light look (45°, above eye level, overheads off, board opposite) and shoot this week's content on it.
  3. Find three posts in your feed where the light feels wrong — can you name why? Once you see it, you can't unsee it.
